The LSOC Approach

Behavior Is Evidence.

Most of the profession trains the sit, rewards the sit, and testifies about the sit. We train something more important: the search that happens before it — because that is where the dog does its real work.

"The sit was not the discovery. It was the communication."

A detector dog does not suddenly recognize target odor at the moment it sits. Recognition happened earlier. The dog met odor, and its behavior began to change — respiration, head position, body tension, movement. The trained final response is the end of a conversation that started the moment odor became meaningful. Understanding that conversation is the difference between simply handling a detector dog and learning to read one.

This is the idea the whole LSOC method is built on: the dog has been talking since the first breath of the search. The handler's job is to learn its language.

Framework

The Five Phases of Detector Dog Behavior

Every search moves through the same sequence. Naming the phases lets a handler observe, document, and testify precisely.

1

Responds to the search command

The dog begins working on cue.

2

Systematic search

Working the area — no target odor recognized yet.

3

Detection

Odor becomes meaningful. Recognition begins.

4

Change of behavior

The tell: respiration, head, body, bracketing the source.

5

Trained final response

The dog reports its conclusion — the sit is phase five, not the whole story.

Why it matters

Phases 3 and 4 are the investigation. A handler who can describe them can explain — and defend — exactly what the dog did.

Framework

The Four Classes of Behavior

Not everything a dog does means the same thing. Separating these keeps a handler honest about what the dog is actually telling them.

  • Intrinsic — what the dog does by nature.
  • Trained — what we deliberately taught, including the final response.
  • Independently learned — what the dog figured out on its own.
  • Handler-influenced — what the dog did because of us, knowingly or not.

That last class is the dangerous one. It's why we train to reduce influence and test to expose it.

Reading the dog

Every dog has an accent.

Reading behavior is a learned skill, built on knowing your individual dog's baseline and watching for change:

  • Respiration — often the first and clearest tell.
  • Head position — where the dog is placing the problem.
  • Tail carriage — tension and interest.
  • Footwork — bracketing, overshooting, source commitment.
"Every search is a conversation. The dog is talking. The question is whether the handler understands the language."
What we build toward

Independent dogs, honest answers

Teach the dog to say "I don't know"

Blank searches and productive failure teach a dog it doesn't have to manufacture an answer. An honest “no odor here” is a trained skill.

Reward the investigation

We never get so focused on the final response that we stop valuing the problem-solving that earned it. Reward is information, not the goal.

The Invisible Leash

Dogs read us. Physical, visual, verbal, and emotional cues can move a dog. We train to reduce handler influence — and test to expose it.

The Scent Board System

Our signature tool teaches dogs to think before they alert — discrimination, bracketing, and source commitment, not pattern-guessing.

Trust, but verify

Reliability is demonstrated, not claimed. Blind and double-blind testing is how we prove the dog works the odor, not the handler.

Handler, heal thyself

Most “dog problems” are handler problems. We train the human end of the leash as seriously as the dog.

Where it all points

Train for the courtroom, not just the yard.

Every exercise is future evidence. The most important trial your dog will ever be part of begins months before anyone files a motion — in how the team was trained, evaluated, and documented.

David's work is grounded in the case law that governs detector-dog evidence — Harris, Jardines, Caballes, Rodriguez, Place, Edmond — and in the discipline of separating observation from interpretation on the witness stand. Reliability that can't be documented and explained isn't reliability a court can use.

"Reliability is not something we claim. It is something we demonstrate."

About the method

What is the difference between an alert and a change of behavior?

A change of behavior is what the dog does when it first recognizes odor — altered respiration, head position, body tension, working the scent. The trained final response (the “alert” or sit) comes later, after the dog has resolved the problem. The behavior is the investigation; the sit is the dog reporting its conclusion.

Why does LSOC emphasize blind and double-blind training?

Because reliability is demonstrated, not claimed. If the handler knows where the odor is, the dog can read the handler instead of the odor. Blind and double-blind work audits that out and proves the dog is working independently — which is exactly what a court will ask.

Is this approach only for law enforcement?

No. The same principles — reading behavior, building independent dogs, honest evaluation, documentation — make any detection dog more reliable, whether it's working narcotics, bed bugs, arson, or conservation.
